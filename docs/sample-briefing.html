Vishing (vishing)

2 script(s), mean sophistication 4.0/10.

What to notice

  • No institution, company, or agency is named at any point.
  • The caller cannot say which account is affected, only 'your main account.'
  • No callback number, case ID, reference number, or verification step is offered.
  • Payment is demanded by pressing one during the call, not through a known billing channel.
  • The threat is vague: 'the account closes and you lose the number' without saying whose number or why.
  • The call ends with 'Thank you. Goodbye.' immediately after the target asks a clarifying question.

How pressure is applied

  • 'This is the final notice'
  • 'Your account is being suspended today'
  • 'If it is not paid today the account closes and you lose the number'
  • 'Press one now' repeated as the only action

What to do

  • Recognize the pattern: an unnamed 'final notice department,' a vague 'main account,' a small balance, and a press-one payment demand. Do not press one. Hang up and verify by calling the institution's official number or opening its app directly. Real providers do not suspend accounts through a robocall that cannot name the account or offer a callback.
  • Recognize an unsolicited card-security call that demands the CVV and a second-card payment. Hang up, call the number on the card or in the app, never read the CVV, never make a verification purchase, and verify transactions independently.

Business email compromise (bec)

1 script(s), mean sophistication 8.0/10.

What to notice

  • The caller supplies his own verification: the letter arrived that same morning and he tells you where to find it, rather than you sourcing it.
  • The callback he offers is to "the number on their invoice" — a document the fraud likely also controls — and he notes that desk is closed.
  • He instructs you to ignore a bank control: "do not override it, just continue" on a beneficiary name partial-match warning.
  • The beneficiary is "Corvus Holdings Ltd" at a new sort code and account, not the Corvus account already on your vendor record.
  • He asks you to read a one-time code aloud — a code that exists to stop exactly this.
  • Every deadline in the call was set by the caller, and the 90-second approval window prevents a second look.

How pressure is applied

  • "the old account closes Friday" — the redirect must land before the cut-off.
  • "Payments after 15:30 land Monday" and Corvus carries "a demurrage charge of 4,100 a day on the containers."
  • "the letter has a same-day clause" — miss it and "the old account rejects and the payment bounces."
  • "revalidation takes five working days."

What to do

  • Recognise the shape: an internal-sounding colleague, a same-morning document you are told to fetch, a cut-off that kills the callback, and a request for a one-time code. Never read an OTP to anyone. Treat any beneficiary name-match warning as a hard stop, not a formality. Verify by calling the vendor on the number in your contract or master record — never one supplied in the letter, invoice or call — and confirm the bank change in writing through a second channel. Escalate to your own treasury or fraud team before keying anything.

Government impersonation (gov-impersonation)

1 script(s), mean sophistication 7.0/10.

What to notice

  • Unsolicited call from "Revenue Enforcement Division" with no independently verified caller ID or official callback number.
  • Caller asks for the last four of the taxpayer ID before the target has verified the caller's identity.
  • Demands same-day payment by phone or transfer and says the online portal cannot move the levy date.
  • Tells the target not to use the number on the back of the card, steering to a supplied settlement desk number.
  • Threatens 48-hour levy, 22% penalty, field visit, and 4:30 auto-referral to prevent checking.
  • Uses badge and case numbers the target is asked to read back, but provides no official notice, portal, or written assessment.

How pressure is applied

  • Levy on the primary account begins within 48 hours once the case is referred.
  • A 22 percent penalty is added after referral, and reinstatement requires a field visit.
  • The supervisor leaves at 5, limiting the settlement approval window.
  • Payment must post by 4:30 today or the file auto-refers and cannot be pulled back.

What to do

  • Recognize unsolicited tax-enforcement call, ID confirmation, badge/case theater, same-day levy threat, no card-back number. Hang up; don't call supplied number; verify via agency's published line and official account; demand written notice; never pay by wire, transfer, or gift card.

Romance (romance)

1 script(s), mean sophistication 7.0/10.

What to notice

  • First contact arrives from a masked number ("UNKNOWN **********42") citing a "hiking group sheet" — the club can be asked whether any such sheet or member named Daniel exists.
  • Voice calls never connect; the excuse ("Rig blocks voice during dive windows, only data gets through") appears only after Nina's call rings out.
  • Intimacy runs on a schedule: "right person at the wrong time" on day 6, kitchen talk on day 14, a money request on day 19.
  • The emergency is sized to be affordable — 900 short, then 850 — and lands right after rapport peaks.
  • Payment goes to a third-party "crew fund" pool account with a reference code, not to a named person.
  • The "offshore bank, transfers take nine days" story makes the money one-way and the promised repayment unverifiable.

How pressure is applied

  • Contract held over five weeks with docked pay — an externally caused, blameless cash crunch.
  • A hard deadline: "The storage unit goes to auction Friday if it's unpaid."
  • Sentimental stake: "That's my mum's furniture."
  • A shortfall sized to be payable: "About 900 short for the month," then a request for 850.

What to do

  • Recognise the shape: a group-sheet or wrong-number opener, a charming remote worker whose job explains both absence and blocked calls, weeks of paced intimacy, then a modest deadline-driven emergency paid into a pool account. Counter-moves: reverse-image search the photos, insist on live video, ask the club whether the person exists, refuse any transfer to a third-party account regardless of reference codes, and treat any request for money from an unmet contact as fraud — no exceptions, no matter how small or how dated the promise of repayment.

Tech support (tech-support)

1 script(s), mean sophistication 6.0/10.

What to notice

  • The caller contacted you first and claims to see errors on your machine — Microsoft and Windows never cold-call about errors.
  • "Windows Security Partner Program" is not a Microsoft department; no case number, employee ID, or callback number is ever given.
  • Red circle icons in Event Viewer appear on virtually every Windows PC and are not proof of a failing drive.
  • He asks you to open "mstsc" (Remote Desktop) and read him a session ID — that hands a stranger your desktop.
  • A free diagnostic becomes a $249 charge within the same call.
  • A "twenty minute" quote hold and a "price doubles" checkbox are pressure devices, not system rules.

How pressure is applied

  • "the drive is already at nine days" — a running countdown the target cannot verify.
  • "Once it hits the failure threshold the session closes and the free diagnostic expires."
  • "I can hold the current quote for the next twenty minutes, that's the most the system allows."
  • "the quote resets and you'd start the diagnostic again" — punishes any delay.

What to do

  • Recognize the shape: unsolicited call, an error code you never reported, a walkthrough of your own Event Viewer, then a request for remote access, then a price. Microsoft does not call about errors and does not sell per-incident repairs. Hang up before the walkthrough; never open mstsc, Quick Assist, AnyDesk, or TeamViewer for an inbound caller, and never read out a session ID. Verify by calling the number printed on your own invoice or the vendor's official site, not one the caller supplies. Check drive health yourself with the manufacturer's tool (CrystalDiskInfo, SeaTools, or the OEM utility). If you already connected: disconnect the session, run a full malware scan, change passwords from a different device, and watch bank and card statements.
